Who May Benefit From Mediation?

Mediation may be appropriate for individuals, families, organizations, and communities seeking a structured conversation around conflict, communication, decision-making, or unresolved concerns.

  • Family and co-parenting conflicts
  • Workplace disagreements
  • Community disputes
  • Interpersonal conflicts
  • Organizational disagreements
  • Professional collaboration concerns

When Mediation May Not Be Appropriate

Mediation is voluntary and may not be suitable in every circumstance. Situations involving immediate safety concerns, coercion, unwillingness to participate, or the need for additional legal, therapeutic, or protective support may require alternative approaches or additional services.

Each inquiry is considered individually. The purpose of the inquiry process is to determine whether mediation may be appropriate and what next steps may be helpful.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is mediation?

Mediation is a voluntary process in which a neutral third party helps participants engage in constructive conversation regarding a conflict, disagreement, or important decision.

Unlike a judge, arbitrator, or decision-maker, the mediator does not determine who is right or wrong and does not impose outcomes. Participants remain responsible for their own decisions throughout the process.

Who may benefit from mediation?

Mediation may benefit individuals, families, organizations, and communities seeking a structured conversation around conflict, communication challenges, decision-making, or unresolved concerns.

The process can be valuable even when participants disagree significantly, provided they are willing to engage in conversation.

Is mediation confidential?

Yes. Mediation is generally confidential. It is important to understand, however, that confidentiality primarily applies from the mediator to the participants.

Participants may choose to discuss their own experiences outside of mediation. The mediator, however, is expected to maintain confidentiality regarding information learned during the mediation process except where disclosure may be required by law or necessary to address serious safety concerns.

Additional questions regarding confidentiality can be discussed during the intake process and may vary depending on the nature of the mediation.

Do I need an attorney to participate?

No. Participants do not need an attorney to participate in mediation.

Attorneys are welcome at any stage of the process, and participants may consult legal counsel before, during, or after mediation if they choose.

Mediation is not legal representation and does not replace independent legal advice.

What happens during mediation?

Mediation typically begins with an introduction to the process and an opportunity for participants to describe the situation from their own perspectives.

The mediator facilitates conversation, encourages direct communication, and helps participants explore concerns, interests, misunderstandings, and possible paths forward.

Participants remain responsible for determining what is discussed and whether any agreements are reached.

How long does mediation take?

The length of mediation depends on the complexity of the conflict, the number of participants involved, and the goals of the participants.

Some matters may be addressed in a single session, while others may require multiple meetings over time.

What types of conflicts can be mediated?

Many different types of conflicts may be appropriate for mediation, including family disputes, co-parenting concerns, workplace disagreements, organizational conflicts, community disputes, interpersonal disagreements, and professional collaboration concerns.

Whether mediation is appropriate depends on the circumstances of the situation and the willingness of participants to engage in the process.

How do I get started?

The first step is to submit an inquiry form describing your situation and the type of support you are seeking.

After reviewing the inquiry, you can expect a response regarding potential next steps, scheduling, additional information, or referral options if mediation does not appear appropriate.

Mediation Philosophy

Understanding Precedes Resolution

Conflict often develops in environments where people feel unheard, misunderstood, dismissed, or uncertain. In many situations, participants become increasingly focused on defending positions rather than understanding experiences.

My approach to mediation is rooted in the belief that meaningful understanding often creates opportunities for movement that persuasion alone cannot achieve.

Rather than beginning with the assumption that every conflict must be settled, the process encourages participants to communicate more clearly, recognize perspectives more fully, and make informed decisions regarding their own situation.

While agreements may occur, the deeper objective is to create a space where participants can engage conflict with greater clarity, empowerment, recognition, and respect.
